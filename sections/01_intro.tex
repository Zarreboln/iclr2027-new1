Visual Place Recognition (VPR) answers where a photograph was taken: given a query, a system searches a database of geo-tagged images and returns the place of the closest match. It is the recognition component behind loop closure in visual SLAM, re-localisation for autonomous driving when satellite positioning drops out, and city-scale services that anchor imagery to a map, and the databases involved run from a few hundred images in an indoor survey to tens of millions across a city. Accuracy is measured by Recall@$1$, the fraction of queries whose top return is a correct place, and the question the field turns on is how an image should be represented so that two views of one place come out close together while two different places do not.

The dominant answer trains that representation. \citet{netvlad} made soft assignment to a codebook differentiable and learned it from weakly supervised place labels, and the template has been followed since: classification over geographic cells \citep{cosplace,eigenplaces}, curated labels at scale \citep{gsvcities}, feed-forward pooling \citep{mixvpr}, and, on top of frozen DINOv2 \citep{dinov2} tokens, optimal transport \citep{salad}, learned queries \citep{boq} and adapters \citep{selavpr}. Each step buys accuracy with annotation: the GSV-Cities line trains on about half a million street-level photographs across sixty thousand labelled places \citep{gsvcities}, the SF-XL line on forty million and more \citep{cosplace,eigenplaces}, and the strongest recent systems pool several such corpora at once \citep{megaloc,selavprpp}. A second, smaller line avoids the annotation entirely and reads a frozen foundation model instead, aggregating its patch tokens against a fitted vocabulary \citep{anyloc}, over segment proposals \citep{segvlad}, or at keypoints selected by the class token's attention \citep{effovpr}.

However, both lines settle what an image contributes using something that comes from outside the pair being compared, and Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows what that costs. The first panel is the supervised recipe: an image is related to a labelled corpus, so the resulting features carry the imagery that corpus happened to contain. They are strong where that imagery is dense, and the scatter below shows how far they fall away from it, every trained marker sitting high on the urban axis and low on the aerial one, with marker area recording the millions of labels each of them consumed. The second panel is the frozen recipe: a patch is related to a criterion fixed elsewhere, an attention map trained to find the object in a web photograph, a codebook estimated on another collection, a segmenter's notion of a thing. None of these was ever asked which parts of a scene identify a place, and the two questions separate: one window in a facade of identical windows is locally striking and is kept, yet it cannot tell this building from its neighbour, while a plain strip of pavement that occurs once may be the only thing in the frame that can. On street view the two answers agree often enough that the substitution is cheap. Away from it they come apart, and selecting patches by attention then scores below using no criterion at all (\S\ref{sec:ood}). This is why the frozen line trails on accuracy despite paying nothing for data.

We therefore take that decision back inside the pair being compared, which is the third panel of Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}: patches are related to each other, and nothing else is consulted. \method{} reads two tables of patch similarity from one frozen forward pass. Within an image, thresholding its self-similarity and summing along a row counts how many of the image's own patches resemble a given one, and the reciprocal of that count is the patch's weight, the closed-form solution of a classical democratic-aggregation condition rather than a design choice, so a facade of identical windows contributes as much as a doorway seen once. Between two images, the cross-similarity table is read twice: contracted from both sides it collapses to one vector per image and scans a database, and taken as each query patch's best match it does not collapse and re-ranks a shortlist. The frozen network is never touched, no vocabulary is estimated beyond the deployment database itself, and one set of constants serves every benchmark we try. That single setting already answers both halves of the split above: on eight urban benchmarks it averages $93.3$ Recall@$1$, level with the supervised systems whose corpora were collected for exactly this terrain, and on nine environments off street view, from degraded corridors and caves to indoor malls, aerial strips and a mid-Atlantic seabed, it averages $76.6$ where the best of those systems reaches $65.1$.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
\item We free VPR from its dependence on annotated data, and from any criterion imported from outside the pair of images being compared, with a method that is simple and effective: two tables of patch similarity read off one frozen forward pass, a per-patch weight that follows in closed form from a classical aggregation condition, and nothing fitted anywhere between the backbone and the ranking.
\item We are accurate in domain and out of it at once, under one configuration held fixed throughout. In domain we average $93.3$ Recall@$1$ over eight urban benchmarks, ahead of every training-free method there and level with supervised systems trained on tens of millions of labels. Out of domain we average $76.6$ over nine environments spanning degraded, subterranean, indoor, aerial and underwater imagery, where the strongest of those systems reaches $65.1$. The second-stage rule also transfers to other methods: dropped into twelve published pipelines it lifts every one, by $+5.5$ to $+12.5$ R@1 where it replaces an existing re-ranker and by $+9.1$ to $+17.8$ where none was there before.
\end{enumerate}
