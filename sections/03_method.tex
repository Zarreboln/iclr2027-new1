\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:method}, the proposed method \textcolor{red}{XXX} follows a training-free paradigm and requires neither model training nor annotated data. All steps of the pipeline rely on a single patch-to-patch similarity (PS) strategy, expressed in three different forms. The query and the database images are first partitioned into patches, and their patch features are extracted by a frozen pretrained vision model. Based on these features, each image computes the similarity between its own patches (PS-1), from which the weight of every patch is derived (\S\ref{sec:feat}). Second-moment pooling of each image's weighted patch features (PS-2) then yields one descriptor per image, with which the database is coarsely ranked, and patch-to-patch \textsc{MaxSim} matching \citep{chamfer} between the query and the top-ranked database images (PS-3) refines this ranking (\S\ref{sec:coarse_to_fine_ranking}).
\subsection{Problem Formulation and Notation}  % CHEN-CHECK: his note asks for a new title; this is one option.
\label{sec:prelim}
Given a query image $I_q$ and a database $\mathcal{D}=\{(I_d,\mathbf c_d)\}$ of $M$ images $I_d$ with their spatial coordinates $\mathbf c_d$, VPR retrieves the database image that best matches the query, from which the query's coordinate $\hat{\mathbf c}_q$ is inferred \citep{netvlad}. As discussed in \S\ref{sec:related}, most VPR solutions require a massive annotated training set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}=\{(I_t,\mathbf c_t)\}$ of training images $I_t$ with coordinates $\mathbf c_t$ to train a dedicated retrieval model. Training such a model takes from about six hours for BoQ \citep{boq} to about a day for CosPlace and EigenPlaces on the $41$M images of SF-XL \citep{cosplace,eigenplaces}, on up to eight V100 GPUs for R2Former \citep{r2former}, depending on the complexity of the method and the scale of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$.

The proposed \textcolor{red}{XXX} eliminates this training requirement by performing patch-similarity-based retrieval in a training-free manner. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:method}, prior to retrieval, each input image $I$ (the query $I_q$ or any database image $I_d$) is resized to a fixed resolution and partitioned into $N$ patches, $I=[\,p_1,\dots,p_N\,]$. A frozen vision encoder extracts a feature vector $\mathbf f_i\in\mathbb R^{C}$ for each patch $p_i$, so that every image is represented by a patch feature matrix $F=[\,\mathbf f_1,\dots,\mathbf f_N\,]^{\top}\in\mathbb R^{N\times C}$, where $C$ is the feature dimension. Following this notation, we denote the query's patch feature matrix by $Q$ and that of the $\ell$-th database image by $D_\ell$, $\ell=1,\dots,M$. Given these patch features, we adopt a general PS template,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p2ps}
  S(A,B)=AB^{\top},
\end{equation}
where $A$ and $B$ are set to different feature matrices depending on the form of PS being applied. We first apply this template for patch weighting, and subsequently for coarse ranking and reranking, as detailed below.

\subsection{Patch Importance via Self-Similarity (PS-1)}
\label{sec:feat}
When comparing two similar images, humans naturally compare them patch by patch. In doing so, we also implicitly assess the importance of each patch based on how frequently visually similar patches recur within the image. For example, patches corresponding to sky, pavement, or grass tend to appear nearly identical across many regions of the same image, and are therefore perceived as less informative or important.

In this paper, we capture this behaviour through a simple and effective patch-to-patch self-similarity formulation (PS-1). Down-weighting content that recurs within an image goes back to the treatment of repetitive structures in place recognition by \citet{repetitive}, which keeps a few repeated elements from dominating the match. Given the patch feature matrix $F\in\mathbb R^{N\times C}$ of an image, the PS template with $A=B=F$ gives the self-similarity $S(F,F)=FF^{\top}\in\mathbb R^{N\times N}$, whose element $S(F,F)_{ij}$, $i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}$, is the similarity between patches $p_i$ and $p_j$. To count how many patches of the same image are highly similar to each patch, we introduce a similarity threshold $\tau$, fixed at $0.5$ for all benchmarks (Supplementary~\S\ref{app:calib}), and binarise this table,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:demo}
  \hat S_{ij}=\ind\left[\,S(F,F)_{ij}>\tau\,\right],
\end{equation}
where $\ind[\cdot]$ is the indicator function. The weight of patch $p_i$ is then defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight}
  w_i=\frac{1}{n_i},
  \qquad\text{where}\quad n_i=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat S_{ij},
\end{equation}
and $n_i$ counts the patches, including $p_i$ itself, that are highly similar to $p_i$, that is, the nonzero entries remaining in row $i$ of $\hat S$ after thresholding. Since $w_i$ is the reciprocal of $n_i$, a patch that recurs frequently across the image (large $n_i$) receives a small weight, while a distinctive patch with few or no similar patches (small $n_i$) receives a large weight, up to $w_i{=}1$, reflecting its higher importance (Figure~\ref{fig:blocks}). The weights of an image are collected in $\mathbf w=[w_1,\dots,w_N]$ and applied in the coarse ranking and reranking described below; Supplementary~\S\ref{app:ridge} derives $w_i{=}1/n_i$ from the democratic-aggregation condition.

\subsection{Coarse Ranking and Reranking}
\label{sec:coarse_to_fine_ranking}
% Chen's original opening of 3.3 (kept for the record, replaced 2026-09-23 because it repeated the overview):
% As discussed in \S\ref{sec:related}, VPR is essentially a retrieval task \citep{netvlad,anyloc}. We adopt the classical coarse-to-fine ranking pipeline \citep{patchnetvlad,r2former,selavpr}, and achieve highly competitive results even against methods trained on millions of annotated samples. More specifically, second-order pooling of the weighted patch features gives a coarse ranking, and patch-to-patch \textsc{MaxSim} matching gives the fine ranking.
Two-stage retrieval is standard in VPR \citep{patchnetvlad,r2former,selavpr}, but existing pipelines treat each stage separately. Here both stages use the same frozen tokens with the same weights $\mathbf w$: coarse ranking pools them into one descriptor per image (PS-2), and reranking matches them patch by patch on the shortlist (PS-3).

\paragraph{Coarse Ranking (PS-2).}
\label{sec:global}
During coarse ranking, the query is compared with the whole database $\mathcal D$ to obtain the $K$ most similar images, the shortlist, which requires a compact descriptor for every image. We obtain this descriptor by weighted second-moment pooling of the patch features, the bilinear pooling of \citet{bcnn}, which captures pairwise correlations between feature channels. Given the patch feature matrix $F$ of an image and its patch weights $\mathbf w$ from PS-1, Eq.~(\ref{eq:weight}), let $W=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf w)$. Setting $A=B=(W^{1/2}F)^{\top}$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:p2ps}), that is, applying the PS template along the channel dimension of the weighted patch features, gives the $C\times C$ second-moment matrix of the image, denoted $S_C(F)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gc}
  S_C(F)=S\big((W^{1/2}F)^{\top},(W^{1/2}F)^{\top}\big)=F^{\top}WF=\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\,\mathbf f_i\mathbf f_i^{\top}.
\end{equation}
As $S_C$ is high-dimensional, we apply $\ell_2$ normalisation and whitened PCA, denoted $\rho$, to obtain a compact descriptor $\mathbf g=\rho(S_C)$ for efficient large-scale search.  % CHEN-CHECK: bcnn and mpncov are in the bib but not yet Scholar-checked.
Denoting the query and the $\ell$-th database descriptors by $\mathbf g_Q$ and $\mathbf g_{D_\ell}$, coarse ranking sorts all database images by $\mathbf g_Q^{\top}\mathbf g_{D_\ell}$, and the top-$K$ images are passed to reranking.

\paragraph{Reranking (PS-3).}
\label{sec:core}
Reranking is performed between the query $Q$ and each of the $K$ shortlisted database images $D_\ell$ (\S\ref{sec:prelim}) via patch-to-patch \textsc{MaxSim} matching \citep{chamfer}. Setting $A=Q$ and $B=D_\ell$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:p2ps}) gives the cross-image similarity $S(Q,D_\ell)\in\mathbb R^{N\times N}$, whose entry $S(Q,D_\ell)_{ij}$ is the similarity between query patch $p_i$ and database patch $p_j$. Let $m_i=\max_j S(Q,D_\ell)_{ij}$ be the best match of query patch $p_i$, $w_{Q,i}$ its weight from PS-1, and $\theta$ a similarity threshold, fixed at $0.65$ for all benchmarks (Supplementary~\S\ref{app:calib}): a query patch whose best match falls below $\theta$ has no counterpart in the candidate and is discarded. The reranking score $s(Q,D_\ell)$ is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stage2}
  s(Q,D_\ell)=\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{Q,i}\;\ind[\,m_i>\theta\,]\;m_i ,
\end{equation}
which sums the weighted best matches of the query patches. The $K$ candidates are sorted by $s(Q,D_\ell)$, and the top-ranked one gives the location of the query.
