\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:exp}
We evaluate the pipeline from three perspectives. First, we compare it with training-free and with trained methods on seventeen benchmarks, seven of them street-level imagery, the domain of the training corpora, and ten from other imaging domains (\S\ref{sec:sota}). Second, we ask which part of the pipeline the result comes from, by changing the patch-distinctiveness signal alone, by plugging our second stage into other methods' first stages, and by holding the backbone fixed while everything else varies (\S\ref{sec:swap}). Third, we show retrievals on four benchmarks (Figure~\ref{fig:qual}). Every number for our method comes from one configuration; nothing is tuned per benchmark.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\noindent\begin{minipage}[t]{0.57\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\paragraph{Datasets.} We evaluate on seventeen benchmarks and split them by imaging domain. Seven are street-level imagery, the domain of the corpora VPR methods are trained on (GSV-Cities, SF-XL, MSLS), and test viewpoint change, illumination, season and weather, repeated vehicle routes and decades-long change. Ten come from other imaging domains: a railway line across four seasons, indoor, aerial, subterranean and underwater imagery, nine of them the suite of \citet{anyloc}. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the benchmarks under the condition each one tests; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives splits, ground-truth tolerances and the three conventions on which the published literature disagrees.

\paragraph{Evaluation metric.} Following standard practice \citep{netvlad,patchnetvlad}, we report Recall@1: a query is correctly localized if the top-ranked database image lies within the tolerance each benchmark defines: $25$\,m for the street-view sets, $16$\,m on Oxford RobotCar, a $\pm10$-frame window on Nordland, the single paired image on AmsterTime, and the provided correspondences or pose thresholds on the other domains (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\paragraph{Implementation.} One configuration serves every benchmark: a frozen DINOv2 ViT-G/14 (\emph{v2}) or DINOv3 ViT-7B/16 (\emph{v3}) backbone, $2{,}304$ tokens per image, $\tau{=}0.5$, $\theta{=}0.65$, a $2048$-d first-stage descriptor and $K{=}\min(1000,|\mathcal D|)$ candidates; on the street-level benchmarks and Nordland, stage 2 stores only the $1024$ most distinctive tokens per image. Baselines run at their own resolution and shortlist depth. Appendix~\ref{app:impl} gives every constant and the sweep that fixed it.

\subsection{Comparison with state-of-the-art}
\label{sec:sota}
Table~\ref{tab:breadth} compares the pipeline with every training-free method we are aware of, on all seventeen benchmarks. On street-level imagery we lead the best prior training-free number on six of the seven benchmarks and are within $0.5$ of AnyLoc on the seventh, Oxford (Appendix~\ref{app:trained-full}), by $+4.3$ R@1 on Pitts30k and $+4.1$ on Tokyo 24/7 where the comparison is closest, for a mean of $92.8$ against $83.5$ for EffoVPR-ZS. On the other domains we are best or tied on nine of the ten, for a mean of $78.6$ against $67.6$ for EffoVPR-ZS and $65.0$ for AnyLoc, whose first-stage descriptor is $24\times$ larger than ours; the largest margin is Nordland, $+38.7$, whose seasonal change no training-free method handled before. The two rows differ by $1.2$ mean R@1 on street-level imagery and $1.6$ on the other domains, most of it on Nordland and AmsterTime, where the newer backbone's features carry seasonal and decades-long change on their own. The frozen class token and GeM, the two training-free baselines that do nothing but pool, sit at $56.5$ and $62.6$ on street-level imagery: the backbone supplies roughly half of the final number, and how its tokens are read supplies the rest.

Table~\ref{tab:trained} sets the same two rows against the trained methods. On street-level imagery, the domain their supervision is aimed at, our mean of $92.8$ edges the strongest of them, SAGE at $92.7$, and we take the best number on Tokyo 24/7 and on AmsterTime, the benchmark with decades of time variation, without a single place label. \subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:swap}
\paragraph{Which signal patch distinctiveness should read.} Table~\ref{tab:w-signal} keeps the backbone, the shortlist and the scoring rule fixed and changes only $w$. Self-similarity, down-weighted, leads at $93.03$ R@1; class-token attention, the signal of \citet{effovpr}, is the closest alternative at $92.62$; reversing the direction of a patch-graph signal costs $9.1$; token norm, inverse document frequency and spatial verification all sit at or below the unweighted baseline of $87.62$. Repetition within the image is the signal to read, and it should count against a patch, not for it.

\paragraph{Is it the backbone?} To compare aggregation at equal capacity, we retrain three baselines on DINOv3-7B, each under its own published recipe with nothing changed but the backbone. Table~\ref{tab:perdataset} gives Recall@1 on all thirteen benchmarks. Our mean is $82.1$ against $73.1$ for the strongest retrained baseline; we are within one point of the best on eleven of the thirteen and ahead by more than $14$ on three; and moving from the DINOv2 backbone to the DINOv3 one raises our mean by $1.4$ with nothing else changed. A larger backbone helps every method, by $1.4$ to $4.5$ mean R@1, and it does not close the gap. The comparison also carries a broader point. The backbones the field builds on have grown from $86$M parameters in DINOv2-B to $6.7$B in DINOv3-7B, and a method that learns its aggregation pays for that growth again with every new backbone, in the labelled corpus, the GPU memory and the days of fine-tuning that retraining three baselines here required. A training-free pipeline adopts the new backbone by loading it, and the gap in Table~\ref{tab:perdataset} is what that costs in accuracy: nothing.
