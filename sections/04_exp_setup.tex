\subsection{Comparison with state-of-the-art}
\label{sec:sota}
\textcolor{red}{We compare the proposed PaSS with a comprehensive set of baselines under both trained and training-free settings. On average, PaSS achieves state-of-the-art results on both Standard VPR benchmarks and Cross-environment VPR benchmarks, against both types of baselines. Note that, PaSS achieves the best results on many standard VPR benchmarks against trained baselines, which require millions of annotated samples. Detailed comparisons against training-free and trained baselines are presented below.}

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:swap}
\paragraph{Plug-in fine-ranking.} Our second stage attaches to any pipeline that exposes patch tokens: each method keeps its backbone, resolution and shortlist, and only the scoring rule changes (Table~\ref{tab:stage2swap}). Replacing the fine-ranker of SelaVPR and R2Former raises their mean over the 9 cross-environment benchmarks by $+9.7$ and $+5.6$ R@1, better on $15$ of $18$ cells; adding it to eight single-stage methods improves all of them, by $+9.1$ (MegaLoc) to $+17.8$ (SuperVLAD), better on $63$ of $72$ cells. The rule needs no training, no fitted parameter and no access to a method's internals, so it moves between pipelines as a plug-in module; the gain is largest where the first stage is weakest.

\paragraph{Contribution of each component.} Figure~\ref{fig:components}(a) adds one component at a time, in the configuration of the main tables. Weighting the second moment by patch distinctiveness adds $35$ points on Nordland and $3$ on VP-Air; \textsc{MaxSim} fine-ranking adds $18$ and $12$; weighting the fine-ranking by the same $w$ adds another $6$ on Nordland and $11$ on VP-Air. Supplementary~\S\ref{app:components} gives all 17 benchmarks; Supplementary~\S\ref{app:coarsegrid} compares the second moment with the weighted mean of the same tokens, and~\S\ref{app:factorize} gives the factorization behind the coarse stage. Figure~\ref{fig:components}(b) shows what the weight does to the first stage on a query that is mostly sky and blank wall: the ten strongest directions of the unweighted second moment hold $59\%$ of its energy, and they describe the wall; the weighted moment needs $22$ directions for the same share, so the descriptor keeps room for the parts of the frame that identify the place. Figure~\ref{fig:components}(c) shows the same effect in the second stage. Without $w$ the largest \textsc{MaxSim} terms all come from snow and sky, where every patch matches every other patch well and the lines cross the frame at random; with $w$ they come from the tree line and the track, run parallel, and read as a geometric correspondence, although the rule uses no geometry.

\paragraph{Patch importance signal.} Table~\ref{tab:w-signal} keeps the backbone, the shortlist and the scoring rule fixed and changes only the signal that sets the patch importance $w$, on all 17 benchmarks (per-benchmark values in Table~\ref{tab:w-signal-full}). Self-similarity is the best signal on both groups, at $90.3$ and $76.3$ mean R@1 against $88.2$ and $75.4$ for patch-graph centrality and $87.8$ and $71.7$ for class-token attention. Bare \textsc{MaxSim} reaches $81.6$ and $74.3$, so self-similarity adds $8.7$ points on the standard group and $2.0$ on the cross-environment group, with the largest gains on MSLS-val ($+29$) and Nordland ($+24$), where road, sky and track fill most of every frame; attention falls $2.6$ below bare \textsc{MaxSim} on the cross-environment group because it keeps spending weight on such content. Token norm stays within $1.3$ of the baseline; the inverse document frequency and cross-referencing fall below it, and rapid spatial verification gains $1.1$ on the standard group but loses $11$ on the cross-environment group. Self-similarity is also the only leading signal read from the patch features alone; attention and centrality need the attention maps of a chosen layer, which the frozen convolutional backbones of Supplementary~\S\ref{app:oldbackbones} do not offer.


\paragraph{Fair comparison with the same backbone.} To compare aggregation at equal capacity we retrain three baselines on DINOv3 under their published recipes (Table~\ref{tab:perdataset}; Supplementary~\S\ref{app:radar} draws the same numbers as a radar chart). Our mean over the 17 benchmarks is $84.4$ against $77.3$ for the strongest retrained baseline; we are within one point of the best on fifteen of the 17 benchmarks and ahead by more than $14$ on four. A method that learns its aggregation pays for every new backbone in labelled data and fine-tuning; a training-free pipeline adopts it by loading it, at no cost in accuracy.
