\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:exp}
We evaluate the pipeline from three perspectives. First, we compare it with training-free and with trained methods on seventeen benchmarks, eight of them the standard urban suite and nine drawn from environments the training corpora never cover (\S\ref{sec:sota}). Second, we ask which part of the pipeline the result comes from, by changing the patch-importance signal alone, by plugging our second stage into other methods' first stages, and by holding the backbone fixed while everything else varies (\S\ref{sec:swap}). Third, we show retrievals on one query per benchmark family (Figure~\ref{fig:qual}). Every number for our method comes from one configuration; nothing is tuned per benchmark.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Datasets.} We report seventeen benchmarks, from urban streets and a seasonal railway to historical photographs and degraded, subterranean, indoor, aerial and underwater imagery. The eight urban ones are the suite on which the field reports, and the nine environments are the suite of \citet{anyloc}, on which the trained methods have seen nothing like the imagery. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} says what each benchmark tests; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives the split, the ground-truth distance and the three conventions on which the published literature disagrees.

\paragraph{Evaluation metric.} Following standard practice \citep{netvlad,patchnetvlad}, we report Recall@1: a query is correctly localized if the top-ranked database image lies within the benchmark's ground-truth tolerance, $25$\,m on the urban sets and the provided correspondences, frame windows or pose thresholds elsewhere (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\subsection{Comparison with state-of-the-art}
\label{sec:sota}
Table~\ref{tab:breadth} compares the pipeline with every training-free method we are aware of, on all seventeen benchmarks. On the urban block we lead the best prior training-free number on seven of the eight and are within $0.5$ of AnyLoc on Oxford, by $+4.3$ R@1 on Pitts30k and $+7.6$ on Tokyo 24/7 where the comparison is closest, and by $+38.7$ on Nordland, whose seasonal change is the hardest condition in the block. The two rows differ by $2.8$ mean R@1, most of it on Nordland and AmsterTime, where the newer backbone's features carry seasonal and decades-long change on their own. On the nine environments we are best or tied on every one, for a mean of $76.6$ against $69.5$ for AnyLoc, the strongest training-free alternative, whose first-stage descriptor is $24\times$ larger than ours. The frozen class token and GeM, the two training-free baselines that do nothing but pool, sit at $51.5$ and $56.7$ on the urban block: the backbone supplies roughly half of the final number, and how its tokens are read supplies the rest.

Table~\ref{tab:trained} sets the same two rows against the trained methods. On the urban block, the terrain their supervision is aimed at, our mean of $93.3$ edges the strongest of them, SAGE at $93.0$, and we take the best number on Tokyo 24/7 and on AmsterTime, the benchmark with decades of time variation, without a single place label. Off street view the picture changes: the thirteen trained methods above $80$ on the urban block each give up between $26$ and $40$ mean R@1 on moving to the environments, while ours gives up $16.7$, and we take the best number on seven of the nine environments and are within $3$ points of the best on the other two, for a mean of $76.6$ against $65.1$ for MegaLoc, the strongest trained method. Figure~\ref{fig:qual} shows one query per benchmark family: where the trained methods return a different place under seasonal change, illumination change or a degraded interior, the pipeline returns the correct image at rank one.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:swap}
\paragraph{Which signal patch importance should read.} Table~\ref{tab:w-signal} keeps the backbone, the shortlist and the scoring rule fixed and changes only $w$. Self-similarity, down-weighted, leads at $93.03$ R@1; class-token attention, the signal of \citet{effovpr}, is the closest alternative at $92.62$; reversing the direction of a patch-graph signal costs $9.1$; token norm, inverse document frequency and spatial verification all sit at or below the unweighted baseline of $87.62$. Repetition within the image is the signal to read, and it should count against a patch, not for it.

\paragraph{Is it the backbone?} To compare aggregation at equal capacity, we retrain three baselines on DINOv3-7B, each under its own published recipe with nothing changed but the backbone. Table~\ref{tab:perdataset} gives Recall@1 on all thirteen benchmarks. Our mean is $82.1$ against $73.1$ for the strongest retrained baseline; we are within one point of the best on eleven of the thirteen and ahead by more than $14$ on three; and moving from the DINOv2 backbone to the DINOv3 one raises our mean by $1.4$ with nothing else changed. A larger backbone helps every method, by $1.4$ to $4.5$ mean R@1, and it does not close the gap. The comparison also carries a broader point. The backbones the field builds on have grown from $86$M parameters in DINOv2-B to $6.7$B in DINOv3-7B, and a method that learns its aggregation pays for that growth again with every new backbone, in the labelled corpus, the GPU memory and the days of fine-tuning that retraining three baselines here required. A training-free pipeline adopts the new backbone by loading it, and the gap in Table~\ref{tab:perdataset} is what that costs in accuracy: nothing.
